# Third-party evidence

Plays and priorities rest on the company's own evidence first. Third-party research explains why a mechanism should work, so a reader can trust the play before the company's own results exist. This file sets the standard for that research and holds a starter library.

## What counts

Use, in this order of preference:

1. **Analyst and consultancy research with stated data.** Gartner, Forrester, IDC, McKinsey, Bain, BCG, with a sample size or method named.
2. **Academic and research institutes.** Peer-reviewed studies, Harvard Business Review research articles, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute and the LinkedIn B2B Institute, Baymard Institute.
3. **Public datasets.** Government statistics, standards bodies, certificate transparency logs, public filings.
4. **Vendor research.** Surveys and data published by a vendor about its own market (G2, TrustRadius, Gong, HubSpot), or studies a vendor paid for (a Forrester Total Economic Impact study, for example). Allowed only with the label `vendor-own` or `vendor-commissioned` next to the citation.

Do not use: undated statistics, figures with no reachable source, listicles that cite other listicles, and any number recalled from memory.

## Rules

- **Open it before citing it.** Cite only a source fetched in this run, or an entry in the library below that was verified on its stated date. Quote the figure as the source states it.
- **Primary when possible.** If the figure comes from a secondary report (a news article on a Gartner survey, for example), name the primary source and say the citation is secondary.
- **Paywalls.** Gartner and Forrester often publish only a summary or press release. Cite the public summary and say so. Never fill in figures from a report that could not be read.
- **Fit the claim.** The citation must support the mechanism the play depends on, for the right kind of buyer (B2B, the right size, the right region where it matters). A consumer statistic does not support a B2B play.
- **Mechanism, not promise.** Use research to explain why a step should work. Never convert a benchmark into the company's expected lift. Expected lift stays low, mid and high reasoning from the company's own baseline, or "unknown".
- **When nothing credible exists, say so.** Write "no third-party evidence found" and keep the play if the company's own evidence supports it.
- **No competitor's research about its own category.** Do not cite research published by a direct competitor of the company being audited, even with a vendor label. For example, do not cite Gong data in an audit of Outreach.
- **Recency.** Prefer research from the last three years. Older entries are flagged in their notes; use them only when no newer source covers the mechanism. The verify pass re-opens every citation regardless of age.
- **Date everything.** Each citation carries the publication year and the date it was checked. Prefer research from the last three years; older canonical studies are allowed with the year shown.

## Citation format

In a play or priority: `[E-07]` for a library entry, or a full inline citation:

`Gartner, "The B2B Buying Journey", 2024, gartner.com/..., checked 2026-11-15 (analyst, public summary)`

In the report's Sources section, list every third-party citation with its type label.

### Statistics to cite with care

These widely repeated figures are often misquoted. Use them only as described here, or not at all.

1. HBR "Short Life of Online Sales Leads" (2011). It does not test a five-minute window; its fastest bucket is "within an hour". The 7x compares one hour with "an hour later", and the 60x compares with 24+ hours. Both are likelihood ratios with no baseline. The 42 hours is a mean among companies that responded within 30 days, which excludes the 23% that never replied. The "21x within 5 minutes" and "100x" figures come from a separate 2007 MIT/InsideSales study and are often wrongly attributed to HBR.
2. Gartner rep-free preference has several numbers from different surveys: 33% seller-free (2020 release), 61% (2025 release, 2024 fieldwork), 67% (2026 release, 2025 fieldwork), plus 75% and 43% attributed to Gartner on non-press pages. Always cite the year and sample. Gartner's own two 2026 releases on the same fieldwork give the sample as 646 and 645.
3. Gartner 17% is time with all suppliers combined. The "5 to 6% per supplier" figure is an extrapolation in secondary summaries.
4. 95-5 rule. The source says "up to 95%" and calls it a heuristic, "not meant to be a precise rule". It is derived from purchase intervals, not from measurement in a given market. Category inter-purchase time changes the ratio.
5. McKinsey "40% more revenue from personalization" (2021) is consumer research, and the text says faster-growing companies "drive 40 percent more of their revenue from personalization". It does not say personalization raises revenue by 40%.
6. Gartner $12.9M is "at least ... on average" from 2020 research. Secondary sites restate it as a current or US-only figure.
7. Self-service versus rep-led outcomes. Gartner 2022 data show self-navigated buyers more often reached high-quality deals (65% vs 24%), and the same research found digital commerce buyers more likely to report purchase regret. Gartner 2025-2026 releases show buyers still want sellers for fit questions and to validate AI output. Citing only one side misrepresents Gartner.
8. Gong multi-threading figures are vendor-own platform data and correlational. Different Gong posts give 67% more contacts, 2x contacts, and +130% win rate on different datasets and years.
9. Binet & Field B2B budget split. 46/54 is widely quoted, but the opened analyst summary (Forrester, 2019) says "roughly 50:50". Not verified from a primary this run.
10. G2 shortlist influence. In 2025, chatbots were the top source at 17% versus 15% for review sites: a plurality, not a majority. G2 data cover software buyers only, and G2 has a commercial interest in review-site findings.
11. Baymard forced account creation. The pages opened show 18%; a search snippet attributed 19% to Baymard. Use 18% and note it is ecommerce shopper data.
12. Gartner "2.5x", "3x", "2x" multiples are self-reported deal-quality or growth-goal outcomes from survey respondents, not observed revenue.
